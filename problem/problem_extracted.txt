2027 MATHEMATICAL CONTEST IN MODELING — PRACTICE
Problem C: On Time, On Paper
Which Airports Are Reliable When Some Flights Never Arrive?
Background
A flight arriving 12 minutes late may count as on time, while one arriving 16 minutes late may not. A
flight canceled before departure has no arrival delay at all; a diverted flight may reach somewhere other
than its intended destination. Yet travelers experience all of these events as different forms of disruption.
An airport may look punctual when measured by the average delay of flights that arrived, even if many
scheduled flights never reached their destination as planned.
In the United States, the Bureau of Transportation Statistics (BTS) publishes detailed records for
reportable domestic flights, including scheduled and actual times, cancellations, diversions, and reported
categories of delay. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) maintains
historical weather observations that can help describe operating conditions. These datasets make large-
scale comparisons possible, but their definitions and coverage matter: reported flights are not every
flight in the country, weather observed at a station is not every condition affecting a flight, and reported
delay categories do not necessarily identify an independently verified root cause.
Simple comparisons can also be deceptive. Airports serve different combinations of destinations, airlines,
seasons, and departure times. One may handle many short routes with frequent departures, while
another serves longer flights with different schedule allowances. A comparison of raw on-time percentages
may therefore reflect the mix of flights as much as underlying reliability. Results can even change when
the same data are grouped differently.
Suppose a hypothetical Air Travel Reliability Council wants a public scorecard for domestic U.S.
air travel in 2027. Rather than naming the airport with the highest published on-time percentage,
the council wants to understand how dependable a planned flight is, how that dependability changes
across situations, and how confidently the evidence supports its conclusions. You should use 2023–2025
as a historical core and may use available 2026 records for later-period testing. The council has not
provided passenger itineraries, individual ticket prices, or a complete record of missed connections; any
such impacts would require estimates or additional evidence.
The council asks your team:
When the data say a flight is “on time,” how much reliability can a traveler actually
count on?
Your Challenge
Develop a data-driven framework for evaluating, explaining, and predicting flight reliability.
Choose at least eight U.S. airports with meaningfully different operating environments, and justify
your selection. Use publicly available flight records and any suitable supplementary evidence. Clearly
identify the observation unit, the population to which your findings apply, and the information that
would have been available when each prediction or decision was made. Do not treat missing arrival
times as ordinary numerical missing values without investigating their causes.

1. Make the flights that disappear count. Prepare and audit your flight-level data. Distinguish on-
time arrivals, late arrivals, cancellations, diversions, and records with incomplete or inconsistent fields.
Develop a defensible measure, or set of measures, of reliability from a traveler’s perspective. Explain the
role of thresholds, denominators, and the treatment of flights without a normal arrival time. Show how
alternative reasonable definitions affect the apparent performance of your selected airports. Identify
data limitations that cannot be solved by cleaning alone.
2. Look behind the rankings. Compare the selected airports across seasons, times of day, airlines,
and route characteristics. Separate changes associated with flight mix from differences that remain
after appropriate adjustment. Investigate whether airport rankings reverse, or become statistically
indistinguishable, under different comparisons. Quantify variation and uncertainty rather than reporting
a single league table. Explain at least one non-obvious pattern and show how you checked that it is not
merely a consequence of aggregation or reporting practices.
3. Predict disruption before it happens. Build a model that estimates the risk of an unsatisfactory
journey outcome for a scheduled flight, such as cancellation, diversion, or a substantial arrival delay.
Decide which outcomes should be predicted jointly and which require separate treatment. Evaluate
the model on periods not used for fitting, including a later period if the data permit. Compare with
a transparent baseline, assess probability calibration as well as discrimination or error, and investigate
where the model fails. Explain how predictive performance changes if the available information is
restricted to what a traveler could know before departure.
4. Test the forecast on diﬀicult days. Examine how reliability changes during unusually disrup-
tive periods. Use observed events where credible records are available, and construct clearly labeled
scenarios for 2027 if needed. Consider the effects of adverse weather, intense travel demand, or unusual
concentrations of cancellations. Determine whether the model systematically underestimates rare but se-
rious disruptions. Provide uncertainty ranges and sensitivity tests for your conclusions. Explain which
scenario results represent interpolation from historical experience and which depend on assumptions
outside the observed data.
5. Tell travelers something the averages cannot. Design a concise 2027 reliability scorecard
that communicates your findings without giving a false impression of precision. Show how advice might
differ for two travelers with contrasting priorities, such as minimizing the chance of cancellation versus
avoiding long delays. Identify an additional original insight revealed by your analysis and explain how an
airline, airport, or traveler could use it. Recommend what new data would most improve the scorecard,
and describe a plan for checking whether its guidance remains reliable over time.
What to Submit
Submit an English mathematical modeling report of no more than 25 pages for this practice con-
test. Include a one-page summary, data sources and cleaning decisions, outcome definitions, exploratory
findings, model development, out-of-sample validation, uncertainty analysis, and an interpretable score-
card. Include a one-page advisory brief to the hypothetical Air Travel Reliability Council
explaining which comparisons are trustworthy, what your model predicts, and how readers should use
the results.
Important distinctions: On-time performance is not the same as successful completion of a planned
trip; a canceled or diverted flight is not simply a flight with zero delay; an average over completed flights
can hide selection effects; delay categories are not a complete causal explanation; flight-level reliability is
not passenger-level experience; and correlation or prediction does not establish that changing an airport’s
operations would cause a predicted improvement. Separate observed records, inferred quantities, and
hypothetical 2027 scenarios.
